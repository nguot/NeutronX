Now I have the full picture. Here's what actually matters:

  ---
  The Core Insight First

  Every filler strategy reduces to the same equation:

  profit = auctionPrice (what you receive in input tokens)
         - cost of sourcing output tokens
         - gas

  Your three current fillers only cover three ways to source output tokens: own inventory, Uniswap route, CoW match. Here
   are the real strategies that go beyond that.

  ---
  Strategy 1: CEX-DEX Hedge Filler

  What it is: You see the Dutch auction price on-chain. You check the same pair on Binance/Coinbase. If auctionPrice >
  CEX ask price, fill on-chain and simultaneously hedge on CEX.

  Concrete example:
  - Order: sell 10 ETH, get USDC. Auction currently offers 3020 USDC/ETH.
  - Binance spot: ETH/USDC ask = 3010 USDC.
  - Fill on-chain: deliver 10 ETH, receive 30200 USDC.
  - Simultaneously sell 10 ETH on Binance: pay 30100 USDC.
  - Profit: 100 USDC - gas.

  Relevant repos:

  solidquant/cex-dex-arb-research (https://github.com/solidquant/cex-dex-arb-research) — TypeScript research template
  that connects to real-time Binance websocket feeds and Uniswap V3 state simultaneously. Has the price comparison loop
  you'd plug auctionPrice into instead of the DEX pool price.

  Risks that Frontier Research covers (A Tale of Two Arbitrages (https://frontier.tech/a-tale-of-two-arbitrages)):
  - Inventory risk: between the time you fill on-chain and your CEX hedge executes, ETH price moves
  - Inclusion risk: your on-chain tx doesn't land but your CEX sell did → naked short

  How to adapt to NeutronX: Replace the "DEX price" signal in the strategy with currentAuctionPrice =
  decayCursor.resolve(currentBlock). If auctionPrice > cexAsk + gasCost, register and fill. The CEX hedge runs async in
  parallel.

  ---
  Strategy 2: Backrun Filler (Atomic Bundle)

  What it is: When you fill a large order by buying output tokens from a DEX pool, you move that pool's price. The pool
  is now mispriced vs other pools. You atomically bundle: [fill tx, arb backrun tx] via Flashbots — one transaction pays
  for the other.

  Concrete example:
  - Order: swap USDC → WETH. You fill it by buying WETH from Uniswap V3 (0.05% pool). Your buy moves pool price up.
  - Uniswap V2 still has old WETH price (lower).
  - Bundle: tx1 = fill order (buy WETH from V3, deliver to swapper), tx2 = arb (buy WETH from V2, sell back to V3 now at
  higher price).
  - You profit from fill spread + arb profit in one atomic transaction.

  Relevant repos:

  flashbots/simple-blind-arbitrage (https://github.com/flashbots/simple-blind-arbitrage) — Solidity + TypeScript. Submits
   atomic two-tx bundles to Flashbots: detect price gap → submit [swap1, swap2] as a bundle. The "blind" part means it
  works without knowing exact tx content — useful because NeutronX partial fills can be detected as pending txs.

  flashbots/mev-share-client-ts (https://github.com/flashbots/mev-share-client-ts) — TypeScript client for MEV-Share.
  Subscribe to the event stream, backrun any order fill that moves a pool. This is literally built for this.

  How to adapt to NeutronX: Wrap your executor.ts's executePartialChunk() call in a Flashbots bundle. In the same bundle,
   add a second tx that does the cross-pool arb. If the arb is profitable enough, gas is covered. Your fill becomes free.

  ---
  Strategy 3: Flash Loan Filler (Zero Capital Required)

  What it is: Take a flash loan for the output tokens, fill the order (receive input tokens), repay the flash loan from
  the input tokens. No inventory needed.

  Concrete example:
  - Order: sell 100 WETH, receive USDC.
  - Flash loan: borrow 100 WETH from Aave (0.09% fee = 0.09 WETH).
  - Fill: deliver 100 WETH to swapper, receive USDC (at auction price).
  - Swap USDC back to WETH using the received USDC via Uniswap.
  - Repay: 100.09 WETH to Aave.
  - Profit: leftover WETH = (auctionPrice - market price) spread - 0.09% fee.

  Relevant repos:

  SimSimButDifferent/UniV3FlashSwapDualArbBot (https://github.com/SimSimButDifferent/UniV3FlashSwapDualArbBot) — Solidity
   + TypeScript. Uses Uniswap V3 flash swaps (free flash loans within a single tx). Scans fee-tier pools for
  discrepancies, executes via flash swap. The contract structure is directly reusable: replace "arb between pools" with
  "fill NeutronX order".

  Key advantage for NeutronX: Your WhaleFiller currently needs a large balance. A flash loan filler needs zero starting
  capital — it earns only the auction price spread. This is a completely different filler archetype worth writing about
  in Ch6.

  ---
  Strategy 4: JIT (Just-in-Time) Liquidity Filler

  What it is: You act as both LP and filler. Right before executing a fill through a Uniswap V3 pool, you add a highly
  concentrated liquidity position around the current price. Your fill trades go through your own LP position, collecting
  the fee. You then remove liquidity immediately after.

  Why it works: A 0.05% fee on 100k USDC fill = 50 USDC. If your LP is tightly concentrated you capture 80%+ of that fee
  even with only a few seconds of liquidity.

  Source: Uniswap's JIT liquidity blog (https://blog.uniswap.org/jit-liquidity) — confirms this is a real, profitable
  strategy used by a handful of sophisticated bots.

  Difficulty: High. Requires Uniswap V3 position management, concentrated liquidity math, and the fill + mint + burn to
  be in the same block (Flashbots bundle of 3 txs).

  Thesis value: This is the most academically interesting strategy for NeutronX because it demonstrates that a
  sophisticated filler can profit from both the auction spread and LP fees simultaneously — which is a property unique to
   combining Dutch auction orders with AMM liquidity.

  ---
  Strategy 5: RFQ Aggregator Filler

  What it is: Instead of computing the route yourself, you query professional market makers (Wintermute, GSR, Jump) via
  their RFQ APIs. If their quoted price to deliver the output tokens is cheaper than the Dutch auction price, you take
  their quote and pocket the spread.

  How CoW Protocol does this: CoW's solver competition rules
  (https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/reference/core/auctions/competition-rules) explicitly allow solvers to use private
  RFQ liquidity. A solver that has Wintermute behind it will consistently beat solvers that only route on-chain.

  Repos: No public implementations (market makers don't open-source their RFQ endpoints), but the pattern is:
  getQuote(tokenIn, tokenOut, amount) → {price, calldata}. Wintermute and Hashflow both have public RFQ APIs.
